Torque per Capita: Research Agenda Model

An interactive implementation of the six research steps in The Last Will Be First and the First Will Be Last. Everything runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded.

Read this first. Every number you see is generated from synthetic data or placeholder assumptions. These are tools for thinking and for testing methods, not findings about any real plan, employer or person. Step 1 accepts your own timestamps so the same code can be pointed at real data.

1 · Instrument one queue

Pick one workflow (for example, prior authorization at a self-funded employer). Capture when each item arrives, when work starts, and when it ends. From those timestamps you can estimate the four measures in the paper.

Use your own timestamps instead

One item per line: arrival,start,end, all in hours from the start of observation. Lines that are not numbers are ignored. When this box has at least 20 valid lines, it replaces the simulation. Set "People at the stage" above to match.

2 · Fit the diffusion and validate it

Parameters are estimated from the first half of the data in step 1. The model then predicts the number of items in the system over the second half. Three predictions are compared with what was observed:

  • Eq. 5 as written: constant drift λ − cμ, reflected at zero.
  • State-dependent drift: λ − μ·min(Q, c). The drift weakens when some staff are idle. This is an extension, not in the paper's text.
  • Discrete-event baseline: a plain M/M/c simulation with the fitted λ and μ.

3 · Make coupling visible

Equation 6: CCoD = (I − W)⁻¹ d. Entry W[i][j] is the share of node j's delay cost that lands on node i. Coupling grows with program time as downstream steps harden their assumptions. Edge weights are noisy, so the spectral radius of W is a random quantity, and the question is the probability that it reaches 1.

4 · Estimate torque per capita

Equation 10: TPCk = μk(Tk) / nk, the output per person at stage k. Equation 11: system throughput is the minimum of demand and every stage's capacity. Change the technology multiplier at a stage and watch whether system throughput moves.

5 · Simulate governance designs

Equation 9: dp = θ(p* − p)dt + σ√(p(1−p)) dB, where p is the share of the covered group in cooperative, health-supporting behavior. Governance changes θ and p*. Claims cost depends on p, plus rare large claims with a heavy tail. Stop-loss caps each large claim at an attachment point, for a premium.

PlaceholdersEvery parameter below is an assumption chosen to show how the method works. They are not estimates. Participation is voluntary in the model: it scales how much of a design's effect applies.

6 · Test the narrative layer

Each statement becomes a vector of weighted word counts (TF-IDF). Cosine similarity near 0 means two statements are orthogonal: they use different vocabulary and do not point at the same ideas. Edit the statements below, one per line as Label: statement. The defaults are invented examples, not real quotes.

Limit: word overlap is a crude proxy for meaning. Two statements can disagree using the same words, or agree using different ones. A real study would use sentence embeddings.

Ethics: the moral case for the queue

The six steps measure queues. This page asks what the measurements are for. It is separate from the model: nothing here changes a calculation, and no calculation here proves a moral claim. It is also an extension, not part of the paper's section 11 agenda.

Each idea below is labeled by where it comes from. Practitioner marks testimony from a registered nurse partner (RN, BSN) with fifteen years across emergency care, cardiac catheterization, patient safety, tribal health and global maternal health. It is lived experience, not a study. Evidence marks a published source, listed at the bottom.

The parable behind the title

"Because no one has hired us." Matthew 20:7, World English Bible
"So the last will be first, and the first last." Matthew 20:16, World English Bible

The landowner hires at dawn, then goes back to the marketplace four more times. At five o'clock he finds people still standing idle and asks why. At day's end he pays them first. Three details carry over to queues:

  • The last were overlooked, not out-argued. No one had chosen them.
  • The owner went to them. The burden of finding them was his.
  • The order of service was a deliberate act of values, made in front of everyone.
Practitioner"The landowner didn't wait for the unhired to make their case. He went out and found them." In a system, that means the institution does the finding, and a person in the queue does not have to be fluent, connected or loud to be seen.

Queue order is a moral choice

Practitioner First come, first served looks neutral. When waiting costs people differently, it favors whoever arrived first. Emergency nurses already reject it: the crushing chest pain is seen before the sprained ankle, however long the ankle has waited.

Evidence Bioethics reaches the same place. Persad, Wertheimer and Emanuel (2009) review the principles used to allocate scarce interventions and treat first come, first served as a choice that needs justification, not a default. Emanuel et al. (2020) apply this to Covid-19 and give priority to those who are sickest and those most likely to benefit. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (1986) requires a screening exam for anyone who comes to a hospital emergency department, whatever their ability to pay. Triage by acuity is law and routine practice, not a novel idea.

The model's bottleneck logic (tabs 1 and 4) says where a queue is slow. This page adds that who goes first inside the queue is a separate decision, and someone makes it whether or not they say so.

Six principles, with their evidence

1 · Priority by verified need

Rank by acuity, risk and cost of delay, measured from evidence, not by how well someone tells their story.

Practitioner The loudest patient is rarely the sickest. The quiet one often is: the elderly man who does not want to be a bother, the woman whose heart attack feels like fatigue and who apologizes for taking a bed. Good triage goes looking for them.

Evidence In a registry of 1,143,513 heart attack patients, women presented without chest pain significantly more often than men (Canto et al., 2012). A queue that listens only to the stated complaint will misread many of them. The same literature carries a caution for tab 3: Obermeyer et al. (2019) found that a widely used risk algorithm ranked patients by predicted cost. At the same score, Black patients were considerably sicker, because less money had been spent on their care. Correcting the proxy would raise the share of Black patients selected for extra help from 17.7% to 46.5%. If "cost of delay" is measured in dollars already spent, it can repeat the inequity it is meant to correct.

Untested here: the claim that the loudest patient is rarely the sickest is a practitioner observation. This page cites no study for it. It is a hypothesis for real, timestamped queue data.

2 · The system carries the burden of understanding

Interpreters, outreach, plain language, and language models on the institution's side of the counter.

Practitioner The longest queue in healthcare is the wait to be understood. Many patients have never had anyone help them say what they need, and the same tools can help the system listen.

Evidence Flores et al. (2003) audiotaped 13 pediatric clinic visits that used a Spanish interpreter and found an average of 31 interpreter errors per visit. Errors by ad hoc interpreters (family members and staff) were more likely to carry potential clinical consequences than errors by hospital interpreters (77% vs 53%). A systematic review found that professional interpreters improve clinical care for patients with limited English proficiency (Karliner et al., 2007). Language models can fail in the same ways, so any use on this side of the counter needs the checks a human interpreter would get.

3 · Measure the tail, not just the average

Report waits for the least-served group, not only the mean.

Practitioner At a tribal health clinic, the longest waits came from distance, language and earned distrust. An average hides all three.

Evidence The Institute of Medicine named equity as one of six aims of quality care (2001) and documented unequal treatment by race and ethnicity (2003). Hoffman et al. (2016) found that many white medical students and residents held false beliefs about biological differences between Black and white patients, and that holding them predicted lower pain ratings for Black patients. Disparities of this kind show up in group-level results, not in the overall mean.

Gap in the model: tabs 1 and 2 report overall λ, μ, ρ and queue length. They do not break results out by group. A per-group wait distribution (median and 90th percentile) is the most direct way to put this principle into the tool.

4 · Knowing creates duty

When the data shows who waits longest, someone owns the response.

Practitioner The party that holds the data that makes a risk known inherits a duty to act. Once your own data shows who waits longest, the harm is foreseeable and a response is owed. The nurse suggests this could anchor the whole paper.

Evidence Door-to-balloon time is the working example. In 1,791 patients with ST-elevation heart attack treated by angioplasty, longer ischemic time was associated with higher one-year mortality, with a risk estimate for each 30 minutes of delay (De Luca et al., 2004). Once the interval was timestamped and publicly reported, hospitals adopted strategies to shorten it (Bradley et al., 2006), and the national median fell from about 96 to about 64 minutes between 2005 and 2010 (Krumholz et al., 2011). The ANA Code of Ethics for Nurses (2015) states the duty in professional terms: nurses work to reduce health disparities.

A caution that strengthens the case: Menees et al. (2013) followed 96,738 primary PCI admissions from 2005 to 2009. Median door-to-balloon times fell, but in-hospital mortality did not change significantly. Measuring a delay does not by itself save lives. The duty is to act on what the measurement shows, and to keep checking that the measure still tracks the outcome that matters.

5 · Consent as relationship

The people measured help govern the measurement.

Practitioner In tribal health, consent means shared governance of the data, not a checkbox.

Evidence The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Carroll et al., 2020) name Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility and Ethics. The Havasupai Tribe's lawsuit against the Arizona Board of Regents, settled in 2010, concerned blood samples given for diabetes research that were later used for other studies without the tribe's agreement (Harmon, 2010). Step 1 accepts your own timestamps. Before anyone pastes in real data, the people in that data should have a say in how it is used.

6 · A human at the threshold

A clinician can still see the quiet patient and move them forward.

Practitioner Triage reads acuity from vital signs and risk, then a nurse looks at the person. A score can flag the quiet patient, and a person needs to be able to override it.

Evidence Two findings above point the same way. Presentation varies by sex (Canto et al., 2012), and algorithms can inherit bias from their inputs (Obermeyer et al., 2019). Neither a stated complaint nor a score is enough alone.

Before pointing any tab at real data

  1. Which queue, and who is in it? Can the least-served people be identified without exposing them?
  2. What is "need" measured by, and could that measure carry an old inequity, as cost did in Obermeyer et al.?
  3. Who owns the response if the tail is long?
  4. Have the people being measured helped shape the work?
  5. Who can override the ranking, and how is the override recorded?

Limits of this page

  • The practitioner testimony comes from one nurse. It is a source of questions, not a finding.
  • The reference list is a starting set. It is not a systematic review and will grow as real queue data and patient voices are added.
  • "Verified need" can be gamed, and any measure can encode bias. That is why principle 1 carries the Obermeyer caution.
  • The scripture is a moral frame, as in the paper. It is not evidence for any model result.
  • Open: the first real use case. It should be one queue where waits can be timestamped, the least-served are visible, and the people measured can help shape the work.

The last, counted and then served first

"Because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." Matthew 25:40, World English Bible

The fluent and connected will always find the front of the line. A just system goes back to the marketplace at five o'clock for whoever is still standing there.
